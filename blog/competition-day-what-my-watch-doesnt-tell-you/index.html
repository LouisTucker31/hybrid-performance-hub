Competition Day: What My Watch Didn't Tell Me

8 July 2026 · 4 min read

After a BJJ competition, I’ll sometimes look back at my watch data out of habit and see a day that appears almost empty. Maybe there’s a bit of walking around, a warm-up recorded beforehand or some elevated stress later in the day, but nothing that reflects what actually happened on the mat.

On paper, it can look like an easy day.

It wasn’t.

It was one of the hardest sessions of the month. The watch simply had to come off before any of it started.

That gap between what the data shows and what actually happened is worth talking about, because it’s easy to let a graph override your own memory of how something felt.

Why the Data Goes Missing

You can’t wear a watch while rolling or competing. It would be uncomfortable, unsafe and unfair on the person you’re training with. So, before stepping onto the mat, it comes off.

That means the hardest part of the day is often the part with no recording at all.

The warm-up might be logged. The walking between matches might be logged. The hours spent waiting around might even contribute to the watch’s stress data. But the matches themselves, the explosive movements, grip fighting, takedown attempts, scrambling and sustained tension, are completely absent.

Sometimes I’ll add the activity manually afterwards, using the approximate duration and my own estimate of the effort. That gives me a record that the competition happened, but it still can’t recreate the heart rate data or show the physical demands of each match.

Either way, the result is the same: a day that demanded a lot can appear in the data as a day that demanded very little, or nothing at all.

If I only trusted the numbers, every competition day would look like a rest day. That is obviously wrong, so the numbers cannot be the only source of truth.

No heart rate data isn’t a training signal. It’s a gap in the data, and those are two very different things.

What I Actually Go By Instead

When the watch can’t tell the story, I rely on the same things people used for decades before wearables existed.

A few honest questions after the session usually tell me more than an incomplete graph ever could:

  • How did I sleep that night, and did I need more sleep than usual?
  • Was I still feeling it in my shoulders, neck, back or grip the next day?
  • Did I want to move again the following morning, or did everything feel heavy?
  • How many matches did I have, and how physically demanding were they?
  • How much time did I spend warming up, staying ready and dealing with the tension that comes with competing?

None of that needs a device. It just requires paying attention, which is surprisingly easy to stop doing once you become used to a number doing the noticing for you.

Where This Actually Matters

This isn’t really about BJJ specifically. It’s about what happens when you let a tool define your effort instead of simply describing it.

The same mistake can happen anywhere tracking has a blind spot: a swim where the watch loses GPS, a gym session where you forget to start the timer, a hike where the strap slips or a workout where the heart rate reading is obviously wrong.

In every case, the temptation is the same: to treat the absence of data as evidence of an easy day.

It rarely is that simple.

The answer isn’t to ignore the data. It’s to remember that the data can only describe what it managed to see.

A missing reading is a limitation of the recording, not a verdict on the session.
